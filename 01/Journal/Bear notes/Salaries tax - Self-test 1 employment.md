# Salaries tax - Self-test 1: employment
Ms. Betty, an U.S. resident, is single, and working as the Asia Pacific quality assurance manager for an U.S. company ('the Company'). She has come to Hong Kong intermittently to visit the Company's customers, agents and her friends but, apart from this, the Company has not carried on any activities in Hong Kong.

The following shows the timetable of Ms. Betty's visits to Hong Kong during the period from December 2015 to December 2016:

10 December 2015 to 31 December 2015 
1 January 2016 to 20 January 2016 
1 February 2016 to 18 February 2016 
1 April 2016 to 21 April 2016 
1 May 2016 to 30 July 2016 (including 10 days of annual leave) 
8 August 2016 to 31 August 2016 
1 October 2016 to 21 October 2016 
15 November 2016 to 20 December 2016

Required

Based on the information provided, determine whether Ms. Betty will be subject to salaries tax in Hong Kong in respect of the employment income she receives from the U.S. company for either or both of the years of assessment 2015/16 and 2016/17.
- - - -
## Answer
Betty's employment is obviously a non-Hong Kong employment. However, she would still be subject to salaries tax in respect of any remuneration for her services rendered in Hong Kong, including leave pay attributable to such services.

In ascertaining whether Betty has rendered services in Hong Kong or not, the IRO provides that if she only renders services in Hong Kong during 'visits' which in aggregate amount to no more than 60 days during the basis period for the year of assessment, such services would be disregarded and the income received for that year of assessment would be exempt from salaries tax under ss.8(1A)(b)(ii) and 8(1B). In Betty's case, since she is an U.S. resident and the question does not seem to indicate that she has neither work base nor family ties in Hong Kong, she should be regarded as a 'visitor' when she stays in Hong Kong. If she stays in Hong Kong for an aggregate of not more than 60 days, she will be exempt from Hong Kong salaries tax. In calculating the number of days of 'visits' in Hong Kong, the Board of Review case D29/89 held that both the day of arrival and the day of departure should be included.

In Betty's case, for the year of assessment 2015/16:

As Betty's total number of days of visits in Hong Kong for the year of assessment 2015/16 are not more than 60 days, she is not subject to Hong Kong salaries tax for that year.

For the year of assessment 2016/17:
![](Salaries%20tax%20-%20Self-test%201%20employment/image%202.png)
As Betty's total number of days of visits in Hong Kong for the year of assessment 2016/17 is more than 60 days, she would be subject to salaries tax.

The basis of taxing the employment income of visitors is usually by reference to the proportionate number of days spent in Hong Kong in the year of assessment to the total number of days in that year. This is the so-called 'time-in-time-out' basis.

Note that in calculating the number of days for time apportionment purposes, either the day of arrival or day of departure is included, but not both. This is different from calculating the number of days in Hong Kong for the '60 days' rule purposes.
