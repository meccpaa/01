# Monitoring the risk profile in a changing environment
## 7.1 Treasury performance measurement

Topic highlights

Monitoring the risk profile in changing financial markets and business is largely done via comparing actual performance against any performance benchmark, such as the approved budget in the business plan.

The act of measuring (and monitoring) performance and risk profile is one of the fundamental responsibilities of a corporate treasury function. This can be problematical due to the:

(a) Difficulty of obtaining information on managerial and risk performance measures

(b) Need to focus on current profit-making decisions rather than backward-looking performance measures

(c) Lack of industry standards by which to judge performance

(d) Moving targets in a dynamic, fast-moving industry

## 7.2 Monitoring and reporting

Topic highlights

Regular reports should include a description of conditions in the financial markets over the period, make comparisons with previous periods and highlight any potential influence on the company's performance that may require adjustments to policy.

Typically the following areas will require monitoring:
- Liquidity and funding risk Interest rate risk 
- Foreign exchange risk 
- Commodity price risk 
- Credit risk Financial structure 
- Market conditions 
- Compliance with corporate policy 
- Financial results of treasury transactions

For each key measurable exposure identified, the report should include:

(a) The current level and nature of hedged and un-hedged exposures

(b) Sensitivity analysis showing the effect of movements in key variables on the company's exposures and positions. This could be done as a prospective income statement effect or on a 'mark to market' basis, revaluing current exposure to current market rates.

## 7.3 Frequency of board reporting

A listed company board would normally meet monthly while small- or medium-sized companies (with only executive directors) may meet quarterly. The frequency of treasury reporting will depend on the nature of the information required in a particular company, including the degree and magnitude of risks. Results of transactions and assessment of risk exposures will be reported to the monthly or other meeting at which financial results are reviewed. Exception reporting may be required between meetings if significant occurrences have, or could have, a major impact on financial results (for example, a dealer exceeding authorised limits).

Some of these reports may be:

(a) Daily reports of any significant impact on the business arising from movements in market rates as they occur, or substantial fluctuations in the value of completed transactions

(b) Monthly reports showing profit or loss calculations, market conditions, performance against benchmarks, sensitivity analysis and compliance

(c) Quarterly reviews of policy in light of financial markets or other external influences

(d) Annual reviews of policies, suitability of treasury objectives and risk parameters, funding plan and treasury performance

## 7.4 Treasury management systems

Most large organisations make use of an automated Treasury management system (TMS). These dedicated treasury IT systems are supplied by external IT companies. An effective TMS can be used to process transactions; assist in cash visibility by forecasting and managing cash positions; assist with financing decisions; and supply reporting information. Such systems facilitate the flow of data internally through the organisation, including interfacing with the accounting and management information systems, and can interact externally with the information systems of the organisation's banks and other financial institutions. In this way a TMS can provide automated end-to-end transaction processing.

In the past the main driver behind TMS usage was efficiency and cost saving. However the productivity gains achieved by a good TMS rarely in practice led to reduced treasury headcount. The benefit of the TMS was that treasury found it could do more with its existing resources.

Financial controls can be embedded within a TMS such as user authentication, transaction limits, authorisation procedures, etc. As well as fraud prevention such controls can assist with prevention of user error which can be a significant risk. Keyboard input errors, sometimes referred to as fat-finger trades, such as the addition of an extra zero to the amount of a payment can be costly.

TMS can offer additional benefits beyond improved efficiency and productivity. Such benefits include:
- Improved financial risk management due to the provision of better information about an organisations aggregate exposures, 
- Greater cash visibility leading to better cash management and potentially reduced cost of borrowings,
- Business continuity can be improved due to the ability of a TMS to be cloud based or at least hosted off-site, making the treasury function more resilient to loss of utilities or connectivity. In addition the standardised nature of TMS inputs makes and organisation better able to cope with staff turnover as new staff more rapidly get up to speed.
- Reduction in fees paid to banks can be achieved as processes become more centralised, for example banks may charge fees related to the number of different entities accessing their web portals. Also fees may reduce as more activities such as maintaining payments templates are accomplished within the TMS rather than using external bank facilities.

In selecting a TMS, key factors to consider (in addition to cost) are:
- Functionality (ability to meet critical business requirements)
- Flexibility (ability to adapt to a rapidly changing environment)
- Ability to integrate with other systems internally and externally
- Scaleability (ability to meet the requirements of the business as it grows)
- Security of information
- Available software support

### 7.4.1 The role of TMS in transfer pricing tax compliance

One specific benefit of a TMS is its ability to assist an organisation in meeting its obligations in respect of tax related transfer pricing requirements.

As we have seen corporate treasury centres that provide cash pooling and in-house bank services use group resources to meeting the lending and borrowing requirements of group companies. Such activities fall under the scope of the OECD’s Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) framework designed to prevent international tax avoidance through the shifting of recognition of profits to low tax jurisdictions.

Under the provisions of BEPS, there will be a requirement to demonstrate that transfer pricing for finance provided by a corporate treasury to group companies is fair. Where a CTC provides finance to a group member, this necessitates the charging of an appropriate cost of finance which includes a margin related to the borrower’s credit risk and to country risk and also takes into account the features of the financing agreement, such as its term. In other words, the interest rate charged should be the same as that which would be offered to a similar borrower in an arm’s length transaction.

A well designed treasury management system can assist in ensuring and demonstrating transfer pricing is in compliance with BEPS. TMS can be designed to assess and document appropriate interest rates for intra-group financing which take into account margins for currency and country risk to meet the requirement for arm’s length fairness.
