# Treasury as a cost, service or profit centre
There are a number of ways in which treasury can be incorporated into a group’s internal structure. The way in which treasury is accounted for internally needs to achieve a balance between pressure to minimise costs and the ability to add value to the group. The impact of treasury activity on the bottom line may not always be transparent and there is a danger that this lack of visibility can mean that the contribution of treasury falls under the radar and lead to an excessive focus on cost control depriving treasury of the resources to provide an optimal service to the group.

## 5.1 Treasury as a cost centre

If the treasury function is established as a cost centre, the costs of the department can be charged to the various other departments/subsidiaries on some basis that is seen to fairly reflect the benefits the other department/subsidiary obtains from the treasury department and the use it makes of the treasury services. If it is not possible to allocate costs on a basis that is seen to be fair, the company may simply treat the costs as a head office expense.

Being treated as a pure cost centre can lead to an emphasis on the treasury function to control and minimise costs rather than focussing on ways in which treasury can add value within an organisation.

## 5.2 Treasury as a service centre

Another approach is to treat treasury as a service centre. Using this model, treasury charges for the services it supplies to other members of the group. This results in a value being placed on treasury activities, and generates an income for the department. As a consequence treasury may enjoy a higher budget for investment in human resources and effective IT systems. Another result is that there will be an expectation that treasury must add value to a subsidiary’s business in order to justify payment for the services it receives.

An in-house bank model can operate a service centre model, charging each group member for the services it provides. In this case revenue generated would include the following:

- Annual fees charged to subsidiaries for accounts held 
- Transaction fees for each payment/receipt processed on behalf of subsidiaries 
- Interest charged to subsidiaries to whom finance has been extended 
- Commission and bid/offer spread charged for risk management transactions

## 5.3 Treasury as a profit centre

Alternatively, the treasury function could be established as a profit centre. This would require the identification of revenues generated by treasury. This may not be straightforward as the primary objective of treasury is not to generate revenue from activities such as funding, speculation and investment, but rather to support the activities of other business units.

The profit centre model may be most appropriate when treasury operates as a highly centralised corporate treasury centre or in-house bank. However there is a risk that excessive focus on revenue generation by treasury could have undesirable consequences such as the taking of excessive risk beyond an organisation’s risk appetite in pursuit of revenue.

If operating as profit centre, treasury revenues could be recognised as follows:

(a) Treasury could charge all other departments/subsidiaries and other head office departments a fee for its services based on current market rates (the total value charged to the group as a whole should exceed the treasury's costs enabling it to report a profit).

(b) Treasury can generate an interest margin in relation to financing activities by charging borrowers subsidiaries a higher interest rate than treasury’s cost of funds, (whether based on interest paid to subsidiaries with surplus funds or external finance).

(c) If treasury is responsible for investment decisions then the return generated, (or the excess above a benchmark), could be attributed to treasury.

(d) Treasury could also earn a profit through its management of the group's exposure to interest rate and foreign exchange risk. Specialists at the treasury department may decide not to hedge group exposure to these risks in certain circumstances. If interest rates or exchange rates subsequently move in the company's favour the benefits could be credited to the treasury department. On the other hand, if such decisions resulted in subsequent losses these would be charged to treasury.

However, there are risks in such an approach, namely that treasury may be incentivised to not hedge in order to generate higher profits. This may lead to unacceptable risk levels. This problem can be exacerbated by the remuneration structures of treasury staff. For example, bonus schemes that are exclusively profit related may incentivise treasury staff to take excessive risks in order to maximise their bonuses and lead to inadequate focus on risk. Thus it is vital that there are controls in place to monitor and limit the level of unhedged risk.

(e) If the treasury department decided to take on additional exchange rate or other risks purely as a speculative activity (for example, writing options on currencies or on shares held) the profits or losses from these transactions could be credited or debited to the treasury function. The board must specify the policy framework within which such speculative trades are carried out and stringent procedures would need to be in place to ensure that these guidelines are adhered to.

The need for well designed and effectively implemented internal controls cannot be overemphasised here. Using derivatives, and the gearing that they offer, it is very easy for treasury to risk far in excess of the initial cash flows invested. Permitting speculative trading activities within treasury also increases the risk of unauthorised or rogue trading. Treasury traders incentivised to make a profit may be tempted to take speculative positions in excess of limits in order to generate greater profits or to ‘win back’ losses.

The following advantages may result from establishing the treasury function as a profit centre:

(a) Treasury function may be able to make a significant contribution to group profit through undertaking some of the actions described above.

(b) Motivation of the specialists employed in treasury may be improved, as they will now be assessed in terms of their contribution to group profit (as compared with the situation if treasury is treated as a cost centre and its costs are simply allocated throughout the group).

The disadvantages of establishing a separate treasury department as a profit centre are as follows:

(a) Investors principally invest in a company based on an assessment of its trading businesses and the risks and rewards they offer. Running treasury as a profit centre and undertaking speculative trading activity can significantly change the overall risks and rewards related to the company’s shares.

(b) The group will incur additional administrative cost if it has to collect data on the revenues of the treasury function as well as its costs.

(c) Problems can arise in establishing a satisfactory charge for treasury's services to other departments/subsidiaries.

(d) The risks of speculation. In practice, many treasury functions have run into difficulties speculating in derivative products and then have tried to trade their way out of the problem. The result has been some disastrous and well-publicised losses. If the group treasury function is to be allowed to engage in such speculative trades it must be closely supervised by trained management to ensure that risk exposure limits are not exceeded. Few, if any, professional investment analysts can demonstrate the ability to consistently predict the movement of financial markets. Therefore speculative trading will be likely to create greater volatility in an organisation’s reported profits.

In recent years there has been a move away from treasury as a profit centre. Perhaps partly as a consequence of the salutary lesson provided by the global financial crisis of 2007/8 on the dangers of the pursuit of profit in financial markets. Recent treasury surveys have indicated that the majority of treasury operations globally are organised using the service centre model with only around 10% operating as profit centres.
