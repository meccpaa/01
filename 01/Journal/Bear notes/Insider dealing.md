# Insider dealing
Insider dealing is a category of '**market misconduct**' under the [[Securities and Futures Ordinance (SFO)]]. The Ordinance has a dual regime for dealing with suspected cases of insider dealing that have been investigated by the [[Securities and Futures Commission (SFC)]] – **a civil regime** and **a criminal regime**. The SFC may decide that a suspected case of insider dealing should be referred to:

- The Market Misconduct Tribunal, under the **civil regime**. The case is heard by the Tribunal, which has the power to:
	- Prohibit the accused individual from taking part in the management of any listed company, and
	- Require the individual to make a payment to the government for the profits obtained or losses avoided from insider dealing.
- The Secretary for Justice, under the **criminal regime**. Individuals found guilty of insider dealing may be imprisoned or required to pay a fine.

## What is insider dealing?
Insider dealing occurs when an individual who is **'connected'** to a listed company has ~[[price-sensitive information]]~ about the company, and uses this information to deal in the company’s shares (or advise someone else to deal in the shares) in order to ::make a profit or avoid a loss::.
- - - -
- A 'connected person' includes directors of the company and professional advisers. 
- reasons to prohibit:
	1. When an individual makes a profit or avoids a loss by dealing in shares when in possession of price-sensitive information, another shareholder or investor makes a loss. For example, if a listed company director buys shares in the company knowing that the share price will soon go up, his profits from share dealing are at the expense of the shareholder who sells him the shares.
	2. If investors believe that insider dealing is common practice on the stock market, they may become reluctant to invest in the market. The stock market as a whole would have a damaged reputation.

## Enforcement of the code, rules and regulations
It is important to understand the consequences of breaking the law or ignoring requirements in the HKSE Listing Rules or provisions in the Corporate Governance Code. The Code is split into principles, code provisions and recommended best practice.

(a) If a company or an individual is in **breach of a law**, such as the [[Securities and Futures Ordinance (SFO)]], a ::crime:: is committed. The law can be enforced through the courts, and the company or individual, if found guilty, will be subject to punishment by the court.

(b) If a **listed company** is in breach of any of [[the Listing Rules]], it has not broken any law.

However it (or its officers) may be subject to ::disciplinary action:: by the stock exchange (HKSE).

(c) If a company breaches a provision of the [[Corporate Governance Code]], which is a **voluntary code**, it has ::not broken a regulation::. However if it is a listed company, it is subject to the HKSE Listing Rules. The Listing Rules require companies to **comply** with the Code **or explain** any non-compliance. A listed company is therefore in ::breach of the Listing Rules:: if it does ~not comply~ with the Code provisions and ~fails to explain~ the non-compliance.

(d) If a listed company does not follow an item of [[recommended best practice]] in the [[Corporate Governance Code]], it has **done nothing wrong**. The Code suggests that the company should explain in its annual report why it has not followed the recommended best practice, but in contrast to a breach of a code provision, this is not a requirement.
