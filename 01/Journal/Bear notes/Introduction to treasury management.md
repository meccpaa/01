# Introduction to treasury management
The treasury function is responsible for the following areas:
- **Liquidity** (i.e. working capital) management: Measuring, monitoring and managing cash flow to protect solvency
- **Funding** (i.e. long-term finance): Creating an optimal mix of equity and debt to meet capital expenditure and investment requirements
- **Financial risk management:** Identifying potential risks and their impact and taking action to mitigate these

The treasury management function within an organisation is responsible for the management of the cash flows of the company, which is the most important measure of the financial health of a company.

Treasury tends to concentrate on the physical flow of funds, cash and financial risk as compared to accounting and corporate finance, which deal predominantly with the recording or evaluation of transactions.

> The essence of treasury management is the management of company cash flows and the risks of these cash flows to determine how much of the company's cash flow is to be placed at risk.  

The stability of the company's cash flows to achieve the company's profit and solvency objectives is the key aim of treasury.

## 1.1 The importance of cash visibility
The term ‘cash visibility’ is used by corporate treasurers to describe a key aspect of the centralised management of an organisation’s cash resources. Cash visibility is required to achieve the most efficient management of a company’s cash resources. If a corporate treasury is to efficiently manage cash it is necessary for it to know both what cash resources the company has and what cash resources it will have, as explained below.

- **Current cash resources**: There is a need for systems to be in place to enable treasury to see cash balances in all countries and all locations. In a small organisation a spreadsheet based approach may be sufficient, but is likely to prove inadequate as an organisation grows in complexity and size. In larger organisations a system automatically retrieving real time information from all bank accounts will be necessary to provide real time information on aggregate cash resources across a range of currencies and locations.
- **Future cash resources:** In addition to visibility of current cash resources, treasury need to be able to forecast the company’s overall cash position. This will require the combination of cash flow forecast from different business units and locations. To facilitate this in an efficient manner an integrated forecasting system capable of combining forecasts in a common format from multiple locations will be required. The knowledge of what the company’s cash position will allow treasury to identify future cash surpluses and deficits and plan investment and funding accordingly.

## 1.2 Corporate treasury centres and in-house banks
A structure where a dedicated corporate treasury function meets a comprehensive range of the financial needs across a corporation’s operations is often referred to as a corporate treasury centre (CTC). A CTC can provide a wide range of treasury functions centrally to all companies in a group and is typically set up as a separate legal entity within a group rather than a division of a company.

CTCs offering a very wide range of financial services across a group are often referred to as in-house banks. An in-house bank can replace external bank providers for a range of banking functions, which could include;

- Cash management 
- Foreign exchange services 
- Risk management 
- Funding and investment 
- Payments and collections in range of currencies

As can be seen these range from acting as a centralised service centre for repetitive actions such as payments and collections through to more strategic issues such as risk management and funding and investment decisions.

An in-house bank can manage the group’s relationship with its external bankers including the operation of its bank accounts, each subsidiary then only maintains an account with the in-house bank which is credited or debited for flows of receivables and payables. If operating in this way an in-house bank can provide improved visibility, control and security, while reducing operating costs.

For example consider a situation where a subsidiary in one country pays another subsidiary based in a second country. This would typically involve at least two banks in the processing of the payment and receipt as well as probably necessitating a currency transaction. Using an in-house bank this transaction would be simplified to ledger entries in each subsidiary’s account with the in-house bank, in addition the in-house bank could also effect the currency translation. In this way the corporation maintains complete control over the transaction and avoids external banking costs.

Additionally by pooling surplus cash in the group an in-house bank can enable the corporation to take advantage of investment opportunities and achieve optimal procurement and usage of capital across the operations of the entire group. The in-house bank should be able to generate an interest margin from the difference in rates if it charges borrowers and pays depositors. An in-house bank will also act as an intermediary with external banks when subsidiaries require finance that cannot be satisfied from internal cash pooling.

Typically an in-house bank will charge borrowing subsidiaries interest and in addition subsidiaries will be charged bank fees for banking services provided. Subsidiaries who deposit cash with the inhouse bank receive interest on their deposits. The interest margin earned by an in-house bank, together with the commission and fee income it charges other group companies should result in a profit. However unlike with external bank relationships these profits stay within the group.

To prevent an in-house bank charging artificially high rates and fees in order to transfer the recognition of profits into lower tax environments, such rates and fees must satisfy international taxation agreements on transfer pricing. In broad terms such agreements require that fees and rate charged by an in-house bank are fair and justified.
